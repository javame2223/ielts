## The Social Life of Elephants | Animals & Geography
Elephants are the largest animals living on land, and among the most intelligent. An African elephant's brain weighs about five kilograms, more than that of any other land animal. But what impresses researchers most is not the size of the brain; it is the complexity of elephant society.

The basic unit is the family, a group of related females and their young led by the oldest female, known as the matriarch. Males leave the family in their early teens and live alone or in loose groups with other males. The matriarch's experience is vital. She remembers the location of water holes that may be needed only once in many years, and studies in Kenya have shown that families led by older matriarchs are better at recognising the roar of a dangerous lion. During a severe drought in Tanzania in 1993, groups with older leaders lost far fewer calves, apparently because the leaders remembered a previous drought and led their families to distant food.

Elephants communicate constantly. Besides trumpeting, they produce deep rumbles, many of them too low for humans to hear, which can travel several kilometres. They show what looks very much like emotion. They greet returning relatives with obvious excitement, help injured companions, and show a striking interest in the bones of dead elephants, touching them gently with their trunks.

Their future is uncertain. In the early twentieth century Africa may have held several million elephants; today there are about 415,000. Poaching for ivory killed vast numbers, and although the international ivory trade was banned in 1989, illegal hunting continues. An increasing problem is conflict with farmers, as expanding villages and fields leave elephants less room.

## Emperors of the Ice | Animals & Geography
The emperor penguin is the largest of all penguins, standing more than a metre tall, and it breeds in the harshest conditions faced by any bird. While other Antarctic animals raise their young in the brief summer, emperors do so in the depths of winter, on the sea ice, in darkness, with temperatures falling below minus forty degrees and winds of over 150 kilometres an hour.

The cycle begins in autumn, when the birds walk as much as a hundred kilometres inland to their colonies. The female lays a single egg and immediately passes it to the male, who balances it on his feet beneath a warm fold of skin. She then returns to the sea to feed. For about two months the male keeps the egg warm. He eats nothing during this time and may lose almost half his body weight.

To survive, the males cooperate. Thousands crowd together in a tight huddle, which may be twenty degrees or more warmer in the centre than outside. The huddle is constantly in motion: birds on the cold outer edge gradually work their way inwards while those in the middle move out, so that each takes a turn in the warmth.

The females come back around the time the chicks hatch and take over, and the hungry males finally leave to feed. The timing of the whole cycle means that the young are ready to swim in summer, when the ice edge is close and food is abundant.

Because emperors depend on stable sea ice for most of the year, they are highly exposed to a warming climate. In 2022 several colonies in one region lost almost all their chicks when the ice broke up before the young had grown waterproof feathers.

## The Changing Sahara | Animals & Geography
The Sahara is the largest hot desert in the world, covering about nine million square kilometres, an area nearly as large as the United States. The popular image is of endless sand dunes, but sand seas make up only around a fifth of it. Most of the Sahara consists of bare rock, gravel plains and mountains, some rising above 3,000 metres.

Daytime temperatures in summer regularly exceed forty-five degrees, yet nights can be cold, because dry air and clear skies let heat escape quickly. Rain is rare and unpredictable; some areas receive none for years. Life has nevertheless adapted. The fennec fox loses heat through its enormous ears, and the camel can go for a week or more without drinking.

Remarkably, the Sahara was not always a desert. Rock paintings in the mountains of Algeria and Libya, thousands of years old, show giraffes, elephants, hippos and people swimming. Between about 11,000 and 5,000 years ago the region was a grassland with lakes and rivers. This 'Green Sahara' was the result of a slight change in the tilt of the Earth's axis, which moved the African monsoon rains northwards. When the rains shifted south again, the land dried out within a few centuries, and many of its people moved towards the Nile valley, which may have contributed to the rise of Egyptian civilisation.

The Sahara affects places far beyond Africa. Each year winds lift hundreds of millions of tonnes of dust from it. Some of this crosses the Atlantic and falls on the Amazon rainforest, where the phosphorus it contains helps to fertilise the soil. On the desert's southern edge, African nations are planting a belt of trees and vegetation, the Great Green Wall, to stop farmland from turning to desert.

## The Mighty Amazon | Animals & Geography
By the amount of water it carries, the Amazon is easily the greatest river on Earth. It discharges about a fifth of all the fresh water that rivers pour into the oceans, more than the next seven largest rivers combined. So powerful is the flow that fresh water can be found on the surface of the Atlantic more than a hundred kilometres from the shore. Whether the Amazon or the Nile is the longer river is still disputed, since it depends on which stream is counted as the source; both are well over 6,000 kilometres.

The river rises high in the Andes of Peru, less than 200 kilometres from the Pacific Ocean, and flows east across the whole width of South America. More than a thousand tributaries join it. There is not a single bridge across the main river along its entire length, mainly because it passes through thinly populated forest where there are few roads to connect.

Each year, rains in the Andes cause the river to rise by as much as fifteen metres, flooding an area of forest larger than England for months at a time. Fish swim among the tree trunks and feed on fallen fruit, spreading the seeds. The river is home to more species of fish than any other, probably over 3,000, along with pink river dolphins, giant otters and the piranha, whose danger to humans is greatly exaggerated.

The Amazon has been a highway for people for thousands of years, and ocean-going ships can travel upstream as far as the Peruvian city of Iquitos, some 3,600 kilometres from the sea. Today dams on its tributaries, gold mining that pollutes the water with mercury, and the clearing of the surrounding forest all threaten the system.

## The Coldest Continent | Animals & Geography
Antarctica is the coldest, windiest and driest continent. The lowest natural temperature ever measured at ground level, minus 89.2 degrees Celsius, was recorded at the Russian Vostok station in 1983. Although about ninety-eight per cent of the land is covered by ice, so little snow falls in the interior that it technically counts as a desert. The ice sheet is on average about two kilometres thick, and it holds roughly seventy per cent of the planet's fresh water.

No human set eyes on the continent until 1820, and the first people reached the South Pole in December 1911, when a Norwegian team led by Roald Amundsen arrived five weeks ahead of the British expedition under Robert Scott. Scott and his four companions died on the return journey.

Antarctica has no permanent inhabitants and belongs to no country. Under the Antarctic Treaty, signed in 1959 and now accepted by more than fifty states, the continent is reserved for peaceful purposes. Military activity is prohibited, and a later agreement forbids mining. Several thousand scientists and support staff live at research stations in summer, falling to about a thousand in the dark winter months.

Their research is of global importance. Ice drilled from deep within the sheet contains tiny bubbles of ancient air, providing a record of the atmosphere going back 800,000 years. It was in Antarctica, in 1985, that British scientists discovered the hole in the ozone layer, a finding that led to an international ban on the chemicals responsible. Today attention is fixed on the stability of the ice itself: if the West Antarctic ice sheet were to melt entirely, global sea level would rise by several metres.

## Clever Crows | Animals & Geography
For a long time the use of tools was thought to be a uniquely human ability, and later one shared only with apes. Then scientists turned their attention to the crow family. On the Pacific island of New Caledonia lives a crow that not only uses tools but manufactures them. It strips twigs and shapes them into hooks, and tears strips from the edges of tough leaves, in order to pull insects out of holes in wood. Different populations on the island produce tools of different designs, which suggests that the techniques are learned from other birds.

The most famous individual was a captive New Caledonian crow named Betty, studied at Oxford University. In 2002 she was given a straight piece of wire and a small bucket of food at the bottom of a tube. Without any training, Betty bent the wire into a hook and lifted the bucket out.

Other experiments recall a fable by the ancient Greek writer Aesop, in which a thirsty crow drops pebbles into a jug to raise the water. Real crows presented with a tube of water and a floating piece of food do exactly this, and they choose heavy objects that sink rather than light ones that float. Crows in a Japanese city have been filmed placing nuts on the road for cars to crack and waiting for the traffic lights to change before collecting them.

Crows also have excellent memories for people. Researchers in Seattle who wore a particular mask while trapping crows found that the birds scolded anyone wearing that mask for years afterwards. Even crows that had not been born at the time joined in, having apparently learned from their elders whom to distrust.

## Songs of the Humpback | Animals & Geography
In 1967 the biologist Roger Payne and his colleague Scott McVay made a remarkable discovery while analysing underwater recordings made near Bermuda. The long, eerie sounds produced by humpback whales were not random. They were arranged in repeated patterns, in the same way as the song of a bird, although on a far larger scale. A single song may last up to half an hour, and a whale may repeat it for many hours.

Only the males sing, mostly in the warm waters where humpbacks gather to breed, so the songs are thought to be connected with mating, though exactly how is unclear. They may attract females, or they may be a means by which males compete with or keep track of one another.

The most extraordinary feature is the way the songs change. All the males in one population sing the same song, but it gradually evolves through the season as new phrases are added and old ones dropped. Occasionally something more dramatic happens. Researchers studying whales off eastern Australia found that within two years the entire population had abandoned its song and adopted a completely different one, which had been introduced by a small number of visitors from the Indian Ocean. Such songs then spread eastwards across the South Pacific from one population to the next. Scientists have described this as one of the clearest examples of culture in animals.

Payne's recordings were released as an album in 1970 and became an unexpected bestseller. The public response helped to bring about the international ban on commercial whaling in 1986, and humpback numbers have since recovered strongly. The whales now face a different threat: the noise of ships, which can drown out their songs.

## The Ship of the Desert | Animals & Geography
Few animals are as well adapted to their environment as the camel. There are two domesticated species: the one-humped dromedary of Arabia and North Africa, and the two-humped Bactrian camel of Central Asia, which can endure winters of minus thirty degrees as well as summer heat.

Contrary to popular belief, a camel's hump does not hold water. It is a store of fat, weighing up to thirty-six kilograms, which the animal draws on when food is scarce. Keeping its fat in one place instead of spreading it under the skin helps the camel to stay cool.

Its ability to cope without water depends on several other features. A camel can lose up to a quarter of its body weight in water without harm, a loss that would kill most mammals. Its body temperature can vary by about six degrees during the day, so it does not begin to sweat until the air is very hot. Its kidneys produce highly concentrated urine, and its nostrils recover moisture from the air it breathes out. When water is finally available, a thirsty camel can drink more than a hundred litres in around ten minutes. Long eyelashes and nostrils that can close protect it from blowing sand, and broad, padded feet stop it from sinking into soft ground.

Camels were domesticated more than three thousand years ago, and they transformed life in dry regions. A loaded camel can walk around forty kilometres a day, and without camels the caravan trade across the Sahara and along the Silk Road would have been impossible. Today they are still valued for milk, meat and wool, and scientists are studying the unusually small antibodies in camel blood for use in medicine.

## Iceland: Energy from the Earth | Animals & Geography
Iceland sits on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, the boundary where the North American and Eurasian plates are slowly moving apart. As a result the island has about thirty active volcanic systems and hundreds of hot springs. The English word 'geyser' comes from Geysir, the name of a famous spouting spring in the south of the country.

For centuries Icelanders used the hot water mainly for washing and bathing. Houses were heated with peat and, later, with imported coal, and photographs of the capital, Reykjavik, from the early twentieth century show a city under a cloud of smoke. The change began in the 1930s, when hot water was first piped to a school and some nearby houses. The oil crisis of the 1970s, which sharply raised the cost of imported fuel, persuaded the government to extend the system across the country.

Today about nine out of ten Icelandic homes are heated directly with geothermal water. Almost all of the nation's electricity is renewable as well, roughly seventy per cent coming from hydroelectric dams and thirty per cent from geothermal power stations. The warm water has other uses. Pavements and car parks in Reykjavik are heated from below to keep them free of ice, greenhouses grow tomatoes and even bananas close to the Arctic Circle, and outdoor swimming pools are open all year.

Cheap, clean electricity has attracted energy-hungry industries, particularly aluminium smelting and data centres. Not all Icelanders welcome this, since new power stations and dams change wild landscapes that are valued for their own sake and for tourism. Iceland's experience cannot simply be copied elsewhere, but its engineers now advise geothermal projects in East Africa, Asia and beyond.

## The Sea that Disappeared | Animals & Geography
In 1960 the Aral Sea, lying between Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, was the fourth largest lake in the world, with an area of about 68,000 square kilometres. Fishing fleets working from ports such as Moynaq and Aralsk landed tens of thousands of tonnes of fish a year. Sixty years later most of it had become desert.

The sea was fed by two great rivers, the Amu Darya and the Syr Darya. In the 1960s Soviet planners decided to use their water to irrigate vast new fields of cotton and rice in the surrounding dry lands. Thousands of kilometres of canals were dug, many of them unlined, so that a large part of the water soaked into the sand or evaporated before it reached the crops. With far less water arriving, the sea began to shrink. By the late 1980s it had split in two, and by the 2010s it had lost about ninety per cent of its volume.

The results were disastrous. As the water grew saltier, the native fish died and the fishing industry collapsed; rusting ships now lie on dry sand far from any shore. The exposed seabed, covered in salt and residues of farm chemicals, is swept by winds that carry toxic dust over a wide region, and rates of breathing and other illnesses among local people have risen. Without the moderating influence of the water, summers have become hotter and winters colder.

There has been a partial recovery in the north. A dam completed by Kazakhstan in 2005, with support from the World Bank, raised the level of the small northern sea, and fish and fishermen have returned. The larger southern part is unlikely to be restored. In Uzbekistan, efforts now focus on planting hardy saxaul shrubs on the dry seabed to hold down the dust.
