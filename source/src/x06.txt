## Exercise as Medicine

A common belief is that exercise is mainly a way of burning calories. Research among the Hadza people of Tanzania, who still live by hunting and gathering, has complicated this view. Hadza men walk about eleven kilometres a day and women about six, yet when the anthropologist Herman Pontzer measured the energy they used, the daily totals were scarcely higher than those of office workers in Europe and America, once body size was allowed for. The body, it seems, adjusts to greater activity by spending less energy on other processes. This is why exercise alone seldom leads to much loss of weight, though it remains essential for almost every other aspect of health.

For people who claim they have no time, there is encouraging news. Researchers in Canada compared two groups of inactive men over twelve weeks. One group cycled at a steady pace for forty-five minutes three times a week. The other did sessions lasting only ten minutes, which included three bursts of all-out effort of twenty seconds each. At the end, the fitness of both groups had improved by almost the same amount.

Nor is it necessary to exercise every day. A large British study that followed more than 60,000 adults found that so-called weekend warriors, who packed all their activity into one or two days a week, had a risk of early death almost as low as that of people who spread the same amount over the week. What counts, apparently, is the total.

## Sweet and Dangerous

One reason why the amount is hard to judge is that sugar appears on labels under more than fifty different names, among them dextrose, maltose, glucose syrup and fruit juice concentrate. Since ingredients are listed in order of weight, a manufacturer that uses three or four kinds can keep each of them lower down the list. Phrases such as 'no added sugar' can also mislead: a fruit smoothie may contain no sugar from a packet and yet deliver as much as a fizzy drink.

Why should sugar in juice matter if sugar in fruit does not? The answer lies in fibre. In a whole orange, the sugar is locked inside cells whose walls take time to digest, so it reaches the blood slowly. When the fruit is squeezed, the fibre is left behind and the sugar is absorbed almost at once. A sudden rise in blood sugar is followed by a surge of the hormone insulin and then a rapid fall, which leaves people tired and hungry again. It is also far easier to drink the juice of four oranges than to eat four oranges.

Artificial sweeteners, which provide the taste without the calories, seem an obvious solution. The first, saccharin, was discovered by accident in 1879 by a chemist who noticed a sweet taste on his fingers at dinner after a day in the laboratory. More than a century later, their value remains uncertain. Reviewing the evidence in 2023, the World Health Organization concluded that sweeteners do not help people to control their weight in the long term and advised against relying on them.

The victory owed as much to strategy as to the vaccine. At first the plan was to vaccinate entire populations, a task that proved impossible in crowded and remote regions. In 1966 an American doctor working in Nigeria, William Foege, found himself with too little vaccine and tried a different approach. His team searched for every case of the disease and vaccinated only the people living around each patient, forming a protective ring that the virus could not cross. In 1978, a year after the last natural case, a medical photographer in the English city of Birmingham died after the virus escaped from a research laboratory in the building where she worked.

## The Simplest Exercise

Human beings are built for walking. Fossil footprints preserved in volcanic ash at Laetoli in Tanzania, discovered in 1978, show that our ancestors were walking upright on two legs at least 3.6 million years ago, long before their brains grew large. The upright gait is remarkably economical: a walking human uses only about a quarter of the energy that a chimpanzee of similar weight spends in covering the same distance on all fours.

The speed at which an older person walks tells doctors a surprising amount. An analysis of nine studies covering some 34,000 people over sixty-five found that walking speed predicted how long they would live almost as accurately as detailed medical information. Walking makes demands on the heart, lungs, muscles, joints and nervous system all at once, so a slowing pace can be an early sign that something is wrong. Some specialists now refer to it as an extra vital sign, to be measured like pulse and blood pressure.

Yet people in rich countries walk far less than they used to. In the United States, almost half of all children walked or cycled to school in 1969; forty years later the proportion had fallen to about one in eight. Longer distances are only part of the explanation. Parents interviewed in surveys mention fear of traffic above all, a danger that grows as more of them choose to drive.

## The Science of Laughter
@INSERT 3
Laughter is famously infectious, a fact that television producers were quick to exploit. Recorded laughter was first added to an American comedy programme in 1950, and experiments have confirmed that audiences rate the same jokes as funnier when they hear other people laughing. The most extreme example of contagion occurred in 1962 at a girls' boarding school in what is now Tanzania. Three pupils began to laugh uncontrollably, and within weeks the fits had spread to about half the school, which had to be closed. Doctors found no physical cause and concluded that the outbreak was a reaction to stress.

Tickling raises a curious question: why can nobody tickle themselves? Experiments in London using a robotic arm suggest an answer. When volunteers moved a lever that made the robot stroke their own palm at the same moment, they felt almost nothing. When the robot's response was delayed by a fifth of a second, the sensation became ticklish. The brain predicts the results of its own movements and dulls them, and tickling depends on surprise.

Not all laughter is spontaneous. We also laugh politely, to keep a conversation flowing or to please a superior. Listeners are surprisingly good at telling the two kinds apart. Genuine laughter is higher in pitch, contains longer bursts and is produced by parts of the brain over which we have little control, whereas the polite variety is closer to speech. According to tests on thousands of volunteers, the ability to distinguish them goes on improving until people are nearly forty.

Most of this evidence shows only that nature and health go together, which leaves open the possibility that healthier or wealthier people simply choose greener neighbourhoods. A team in Philadelphia set out to test cause and effect. They selected 541 abandoned plots across the city and, at random, either cleared the rubbish and planted grass and trees, or left the plots as they were. How much contact with nature is needed? British researchers who questioned nearly 20,000 people found that those who had spent at least two hours in parks, woods or on beaches during the previous week were considerably more likely to report good health and high satisfaction with life than those who had spent none. Below two hours there was no measurable benefit. It made no difference whether the time was taken in one long visit or several short ones.

A grim natural experiment points the same way. Since 2002 an Asian beetle accidentally introduced to North America has killed more than a hundred million ash trees. When scientists compared counties that had lost their trees with those the insect had not yet reached, they found higher rates of death from heart and lung disease in the affected areas, amounting to over 20,000 additional deaths.

Studies of thousands of pairs of Danish twins, who share their genes but often lead different lives, indicate that only about a quarter of the variation in how long people live is explained by heredity. The researchers predicted that the advantage would disappear if these habits changed, and by the 1970s, as younger people moved into separate houses, it had.

The Californian zone shows that such results do not depend on isolation or tradition. ## The Doctor Who Washed His Hands
@INSERT 4
Others were reaching similar conclusions by different routes. During the Crimean War of 1854 to 1856, the English nurse Florence Nightingale found that far more soldiers in the British military hospital were dying of infections caught there than of wounds received in battle. After the hospital's sewers were cleaned and its wards ventilated, the death rate fell sharply. Nightingale was a gifted statistician, and on her return she presented her figures to the government in the form of striking coloured diagrams, which did much to bring about the reform of hospitals.

In Glasgow, the surgeon Joseph Lister had read Pasteur's work on microbes and reasoned that they might be responsible for the infections that killed almost half of his patients after amputations. From 1865 he cleaned wounds and instruments with carbolic acid, a chemical used to treat sewage. Deaths in his ward fell to about fifteen per cent.

It would be pleasant to report that the lesson has been fully learned. Yet infections caught in hospital remain a serious problem, and studies in many countries have found that doctors and nurses clean their hands on fewer than half of the occasions when they should. Pressure of time is the usual reason. Gel dispensers placed beside every bed have helped, and so have simple checklists. When intensive care units in the American state of Michigan adopted a five-point checklist that began with hand washing, a common type of infection fell by about two thirds.
