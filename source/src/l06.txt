## Urban Heat Islands | Section 4
@CTX You will hear part of a lecture on the climate of cities.
@VOICES Lecturer=f
@S
Lecturer: Good morning. Today's topic is a problem that affects more than half of the world's population, the urban heat island.
Lecturer: The term describes the fact that cities are warmer than the countryside around them. The effect was first measured in London in the early nineteenth century by an amateur scientist called Luke Howard, who compared thermometer readings in the centre with those in nearby villages. Today the difference in a large city is typically between one and three degrees during the day. But the important point is that it is greatest at night, when a city centre can be as much as ten degrees warmer than the surrounding fields.
Lecturer: Why does this happen? There are four main causes. First, materials. Dark surfaces such as asphalt and roof tiles absorb sunlight during the day and release the heat slowly after sunset. Second, the lack of plants. Trees cool the air, both by giving shade and by releasing water through their leaves. Third, the shape of cities. Tall buildings on narrow streets trap heat and block the wind. And fourth, waste heat from vehicles, factories and, ironically, air conditioners, which cool the inside of a building by pumping heat outside.
Lecturer: The consequences are serious. During heat waves the death rate rises, especially among elderly people, because the body cannot recover when nights stay hot. Demand for electricity increases sharply, and air quality gets worse.
Lecturer: What can be done? The cheapest solution is to change the colour of surfaces. A white roof reflects most of the sunlight that falls on it, and can be thirty degrees cooler than a black one. New York has painted more than a million square metres of roofs white.
Lecturer: Planting trees is the second approach. Studies show that a street with mature trees can be several degrees cooler than one without. The city of Melbourne plans to almost double its tree cover by twenty forty.
Lecturer: Green roofs, covered with soil and plants, are a third option, though they cost more to build. Next week we'll look at how planners are using these ideas.

## The History of the Pencil | Section 4
@CTX You will hear part of a lecture about the history of an everyday object.
@VOICES Lecturer=m
@S
Lecturer: In this series on everyday technology, I want to turn today to an object so ordinary that we hardly notice it, the pencil.
Lecturer: Its story begins in the north of England in the fifteen sixties. According to tradition, a storm blew down a large tree near the village of Borrowdale, and under its roots shepherds found a black, shiny substance. They discovered it was excellent for marking their sheep. People believed the material was a kind of lead, which is why we still talk about the lead of a pencil. In fact it was graphite, a form of carbon, and pencils have never contained any lead at all.
Lecturer: The Borrowdale graphite was unusually pure and solid. It could be cut into thin sticks, which at first were wrapped in string or sheepskin, and later placed inside a wooden case. For two centuries England had almost a monopoly, and the graphite was so valuable that it was transported under armed guard.
Lecturer: The modern pencil was born out of war. In the seventeen nineties France was fighting Britain and could no longer import English graphite. An officer in the French army, Nicolas Conte, was asked to find a solution. In seventeen ninety-five he mixed powdered graphite of poor quality with clay, formed the mixture into rods and baked them in an oven. The method had an unexpected advantage. By changing the amount of clay, he could make the pencil harder or softer. More clay gives a harder, lighter line. That is the origin of the grades we still use, such as H for hard and B for black.
Lecturer: Two later improvements came from America. In eighteen fifty-eight Hymen Lipman patented a pencil with a rubber attached to the end. And the familiar yellow colour appeared in eighteen ninety. The best graphite then came from China, where yellow was associated with royalty, so manufacturers painted their finest pencils yellow as a sign of quality.
Lecturer: Why has the pencil survived the computer age? It is cheap, it needs no battery, and it works upside down, under water and even in space. A single pencil can draw a line roughly fifty-six kilometres long.

## How Animals Sleep | Section 4
@CTX You will hear part of a lecture on sleep in the animal kingdom.
@VOICES Lecturer=f
@S
Lecturer: Last week we looked at human sleep. Today I want to widen the picture and ask how other animals sleep, because the variety is astonishing.
Lecturer: Let's begin with the amount. The koala is one of the champions, sleeping for up to twenty hours a day. The reason is its diet. Eucalyptus leaves contain very little energy and take a long time to digest. At the other extreme are large plant-eaters that must spend most of their time feeding and watching for predators. A study published in two thousand and seventeen, which fitted wild African elephants with activity monitors, found that they slept for only about two hours a night, the shortest sleep recorded for any mammal.
Lecturer: Now consider animals that can never fully stop. Dolphins must come to the surface to breathe, so they cannot become completely unconscious. Their solution is to sleep with one half of the brain at a time. The other half stays awake, and the eye connected to it remains open. After about two hours the two sides change over.
Lecturer: Some birds do something similar. Ducks sleeping in a row are a good example. The birds at the ends of the row keep the outer eye open, watching for danger, while those in the middle close both eyes.
Lecturer: Perhaps the most remarkable case is the frigatebird, a seabird that can stay in the air over the ocean for two months without landing. Researchers who attached small recorders to these birds found that they do sleep while flying, but for only about forty minutes a day, in bursts of a few seconds. On land the same birds sleep for more than twelve hours.
Lecturer: Sea otters have a different problem, which is drifting away while asleep. They wrap themselves in seaweed, and mothers and young sometimes hold each other's paws.
Lecturer: What does all this tell us? Sleep is clearly so important that evolution has kept it even where it is dangerous or inconvenient. No animal that has been studied carefully manages without it entirely.

## The Science of Habits | Section 4
@CTX You will hear part of a lecture on the psychology of habits.
@VOICES Lecturer=m
@S
Lecturer: Today we're looking at habits, the things we do automatically, without making a decision. Research by the psychologist Wendy Wood suggests that about forty-three per cent of our daily actions are performed out of habit, while we are thinking about something else.
Lecturer: How does a habit work? Researchers describe a loop with three parts. First there is a cue, a signal that starts the behaviour. It might be a time of day, a place, or a feeling such as boredom. Second comes the routine, the behaviour itself. And third is the reward, which tells the brain that the loop is worth remembering. After enough repetitions, the cue alone produces a desire for the reward.
Lecturer: You have probably heard that it takes twenty-one days to form a habit. That figure comes from a book published in nineteen sixty by a plastic surgeon, Maxwell Maltz, who noticed that his patients needed about three weeks to get used to their new faces. It was an observation, not an experiment.
Lecturer: A proper study was carried out in two thousand and nine at University College London. Ninety-six volunteers chose a new daily behaviour, such as eating fruit with lunch, and reported each day how automatic it felt. On average, a habit took sixty-six days to form. But the range was enormous, from eighteen days to two hundred and fifty-four. Simple actions like drinking water became automatic quickly. Exercise took much longer. Encouragingly, missing a single day made no real difference.
Lecturer: What about breaking a bad habit? The evidence suggests that habits are not deleted, they are replaced. The most effective method is to keep the cue and the reward, and change only the routine. Someone who eats biscuits at four o'clock because they want a break might take a short walk instead.
Lecturer: Changing the environment also helps. Studies of students who moved to a different university found that they changed their habits more easily, because the old cues had disappeared. So if you want to start a new habit, a good moment is when you move house or begin a new job.

## The Origins of Writing | Section 4
@CTX You will hear part of a lecture on the earliest writing system.
@VOICES Lecturer=f
@S
Lecturer: When we think about why writing was invented, we imagine poetry, or laws, or letters. The truth is less romantic. Writing was invented by accountants.
Lecturer: The story begins in Mesopotamia, in what is now southern Iraq. By about three thousand three hundred BC the city of Uruk had perhaps forty thousand inhabitants, and its temples controlled huge stores of grain, sheep and beer. Somebody had to keep records.
Lecturer: For thousands of years before this, people in the region had used small clay tokens for counting. One shape stood for a measure of grain, another for a jar of oil. To prevent cheating, the tokens for a delivery were sealed inside a hollow clay ball. The problem, of course, was that you had to break the ball to check what was in it. So people began pressing the tokens into the outside of the ball before sealing it. And eventually someone realised that the marks alone were enough. The ball became a flat tablet, and the tokens were no longer needed.
Lecturer: The first signs were simple pictures. A head of barley meant barley. But pictures are slow to draw in wet clay, so scribes began pressing the end of a cut reed into the surface instead. This made marks shaped like wedges. The Latin word for wedge is cuneus, and so we call the script cuneiform.
Lecturer: The next step was the decisive one. Signs began to represent sounds as well as things. This allowed scribes to write names and, eventually, whole sentences of the spoken language. The number of signs fell from more than a thousand to about six hundred. That was still a great many to learn, and training as a scribe took around twelve years in a school known as a tablet house.
Lecturer: Cuneiform was used for three thousand years, for a dozen different languages. It recorded contracts, medical texts and literature, including the story of Gilgamesh. Then it was forgotten completely, until scholars deciphered it in the nineteenth century. Because clay survives fire, hundreds of thousands of tablets still exist, most of them, I'm afraid, receipts.

## Plants of the Desert | Section 4
@CTX You will hear part of a lecture on how plants survive in deserts.
@VOICES Lecturer=m
@S
Lecturer: A desert is defined as a region that receives less than two hundred and fifty millimetres of rain a year. For a plant, that creates one overwhelming problem, how to obtain water and how to keep it. Today I'll describe three strategies that desert plants have evolved.
Lecturer: The first strategy is storage, and the best-known example is the cactus. Look at a saguaro cactus from the deserts of Arizona. It has no leaves. Its spines are in fact leaves that have changed over millions of years. They lose almost no water, they protect the plant from thirsty animals, and they even provide a little shade. The stem of the saguaro is folded, like an accordion, so that it can expand. After a storm, a large plant can take up about seven hundred and fifty litres of water in a few days. Its roots are shallow, but they spread out as far as the plant is tall, to catch rain before it disappears.
Lecturer: Cacti also breathe differently from other plants. Most plants open the tiny holes in their surface during the day to take in carbon dioxide, and they lose water as they do so. A cactus opens them only at night, when the air is cool, stores the gas, and uses it for photosynthesis the next day. The price is slow growth. A saguaro may be only a few centimetres tall after ten years, though it can live for two centuries.
Lecturer: The second strategy is to reach water that other plants cannot. The mesquite tree sends its roots down to water lying deep underground. Roots have been found at depths of more than fifty metres.
Lecturer: The third strategy is simply to avoid the dry season. Many small desert flowers live for only a few weeks. Their seeds can lie in the soil for years, protected by a chemical coat that prevents them from growing. Only heavy rain washes the chemical away. Then the desert suddenly turns into a carpet of colour, the plants flower, produce new seeds and die, all before the ground dries out again.
Lecturer: Next time, we'll consider desert animals.

## Lifts and the Rise of the Skyscraper | Section 4
@CTX You will hear part of a lecture on the history of architecture.
@VOICES Lecturer=f
@S
Lecturer: If I asked you which invention made the modern city possible, you might say steel, or concrete. I'd like to suggest another candidate, the lift, or elevator.
Lecturer: Lifting platforms are very old. The Romans used them in the Colosseum to raise animals into the arena. Nineteenth-century factories and warehouses had steam-powered lifts for goods. But nobody would travel in one by choice, for a simple reason. If the rope broke, the platform fell.
Lecturer: The man who solved this was an American mechanic, Elisha Otis. He designed a safety brake, a spring that pushed metal teeth into the guide rails on each side if the rope lost its tension. In eighteen fifty-four, at a great exhibition in New York, he demonstrated it in dramatic style. He stood on a platform high above the crowd and ordered the rope to be cut. The platform dropped a few centimetres, and stopped.
Lecturer: The first passenger lift using his brake was installed three years later in a department store in New York. It was a building of only five storeys, and the lift travelled at less than a kilometre an hour. But the principle was established.
Lecturer: Consider what this meant for buildings. Before the lift, few were higher than six floors, because that was as far as people were willing to climb. And the top floors were the cheapest, occupied by servants and the poor. The lift turned that upside down. Suddenly the highest floors, with light, quiet and a view, became the most desirable and the most expensive.
Lecturer: Height then depended on structure. Steel frames, first used in Chicago in the eighteen eighties, allowed walls to become thin. Electric lifts arrived in the same decade and were faster and cleaner than steam.
Lecturer: Today's limits are different. In very tall buildings the steel cables become too heavy to support their own weight, which restricts a single lift to about five hundred metres. Engineers are now using cables of carbon fibre, which weigh far less. And in the tallest towers, lifts take up so much floor space that designers use double-deck cars, serving two floors at once.

Host: It's a great question, because it really doesn't. Archaeologists opening Egyptian tombs have found pots of honey around three thousand years old, and it was still perfectly safe to eat.
Host: So what's the secret? There are three parts to the answer. The first is water, or rather the lack of it. Honey is only about seventeen per cent water. Bacteria and moulds need moisture to grow, and when they land in honey the sugar actually pulls the water out of them, and they die.
Host: The second reason is that honey is acidic. Its acidity is similar to that of orange juice, and most microbes can't survive in that.

Then the bees fan the nectar with their wings to evaporate the water, and finally they seal each cell with wax.
Host: That, by the way, explains why honey has been used as medicine for thousands of years. People put it on cuts and burns long before anyone knew what bacteria were, and some hospitals use a special medical honey today.
Host: One warning. Honey only lasts if the jar is closed. Leave it open in a damp kitchen and it absorbs water from the air, and then it can spoil. And if your honey goes hard and cloudy, don't throw it out. It has only crystallised. Stand the jar in warm water and it will turn liquid again.
Host: Finally, a number that changed how I look at my breakfast. In her whole life, a single worker bee makes about one twelfth of a teaspoon of honey.
Host: Thanks, Olivia. Keep the questions coming.
