## The History of the Postal Service | Section 4
@CTX You will hear part of a lecture about the development of postal services.
@VOICES Lecturer=m
@S
Lecturer: Today I want to look at something we tend to take for granted, the postal service, and to show how it shaped modern communication.
Lecturer: Organised systems for carrying messages are extremely old. The Persian Empire, around five hundred BC, had a royal road with stations where riders changed horses. A message could cross the empire in about a week. But this system, like the later Roman one, was for government use only. Ordinary people had to rely on travellers or merchants to carry their letters.
Lecturer: Public postal services developed in Europe from the sixteenth century, but they were expensive and complicated. In Britain before eighteen forty, the cost of a letter depended on the distance and on the number of sheets of paper. And, importantly, the cost was paid not by the sender but by the person who received it. Many people simply refused to accept letters they could not afford.
Lecturer: The reform came from a teacher called Rowland Hill. In eighteen thirty-seven he published a pamphlet arguing that the real cost of carrying a letter was tiny, and that most of the expense came from calculating and collecting the charges. He proposed a single low price of one penny for any distance, paid in advance by the sender. To show payment, he suggested a small piece of paper with glue on the back. This was the first postage stamp, known as the Penny Black, issued in May eighteen forty.
Lecturer: The results were dramatic. Within a year the number of letters sent in Britain more than doubled. Other countries copied the system quickly. Cheap post encouraged people to learn to read and write, helped businesses to grow, and allowed families separated by migration to stay in contact.
Lecturer: In eighteen seventy-four, an international agreement signed in Switzerland created what is now the Universal Postal Union, so that a stamp bought in one country would carry a letter to any other. Today, of course, the number of letters is falling because of email, but parcels are increasing rapidly as a result of online shopping.

## How Bees Communicate | Section 4
@CTX You will hear part of a lecture on animal behaviour.
@VOICES Lecturer=f
@S
Lecturer: In this lecture I'd like to describe one of the most remarkable communication systems in the animal world, the dance of the honeybee.
Lecturer: Beekeepers had noticed for centuries that when one bee found a rich source of food, many others soon arrived at the same place. The explanation was discovered by an Austrian scientist, Karl von Frisch, who began his experiments in the nineteen twenties. He marked individual bees with tiny spots of paint and watched them through a glass wall in the hive.
Lecturer: He found that a bee returning from flowers close to the hive performs what he called the round dance. She simply runs in circles. This tells the others that food is nearby, within about fifty metres, but it gives no direction.
Lecturer: For food that is farther away, the bee performs the waggle dance. She runs in a straight line while shaking her body from side to side, then circles back and repeats the run. The two key pieces of information are in that straight run. First, its length shows the distance. Roughly speaking, one second of waggling means one kilometre. Second, the angle of the run shows the direction. Inside the dark hive the bee dances on a vertical surface, and she uses gravity to represent the sun. A run straight up means fly towards the sun. A run at sixty degrees to the right of vertical means fly sixty degrees to the right of the sun.
Lecturer: The other bees follow the dancer closely, touching her with their antennae, and they also learn the smell of the flowers from her body.
Lecturer: Many scientists at first refused to believe that an insect could use such a symbolic language. Von Frisch's ideas were only fully accepted decades later, and he shared the Nobel Prize in nineteen seventy-three. Later experiments using tiny radar devices attached to bees confirmed that they really do fly to the place the dance indicates.
Lecturer: Bees also use the dance when choosing a new home. Scouts dance to advertise sites they have found, and the colony moves only when enough scouts agree. It is, in effect, a kind of democratic vote.

## The Development of the Shipping Container | Section 4
@CTX You will hear part of a lecture on the history of world trade.
@VOICES Lecturer=m
@S
Lecturer: When we talk about globalisation, we usually think of the internet or of trade agreements. Today I want to argue that a simple steel box has been just as important.
Lecturer: Until the nineteen fifties, loading a ship was slow and costly. Goods arrived at the port in sacks, barrels and wooden crates of every size. Each item had to be carried on board by hand and packed carefully into the ship. A vessel could spend more time in port than at sea, and loading could account for half the total cost of a voyage. Theft was common too.
Lecturer: The man who changed this was not a sailor but an American trucking businessman, Malcolm McLean. He asked a simple question. Why unload the truck at all? Why not lift the whole body of the truck onto the ship? On the twenty-sixth of April nineteen fifty-six, his converted oil tanker, the Ideal X, sailed from New Jersey to Texas carrying fifty-eight containers. The cost of loading fell from nearly six dollars a ton to about sixteen cents.
Lecturer: Success was not immediate. Ports needed huge new cranes, and dock workers, who feared for their jobs, went on strike. Above all, different companies used boxes of different sizes. Only in the nineteen sixties were international standards agreed, the most common being lengths of twenty and forty feet. Standard boxes could move from ship to train to truck anywhere in the world.
Lecturer: The consequences were enormous. Transport became so cheap that it made sense to manufacture goods thousands of kilometres from the customer. Factories moved to countries with lower wages, especially in East Asia. Old ports in city centres, such as London and New York, declined, because container ships needed deep water and a great deal of space. New ports like Rotterdam and Singapore grew rapidly.
Lecturer: Today about ninety per cent of non-bulk goods travel in containers, and the largest ships carry more than twenty thousand of them. The system is efficient but also fragile, as we saw in twenty twenty-one, when a single ship blocked the Suez Canal for six days and delayed trade around the world.
