Reading images is only one application. A different kind of program has transformed a problem that had defeated biologists for fifty years: predicting the three-dimensional shape of a protein from its chemical sequence. The shape determines what a protein does in the body, and working it out in the laboratory could take years. Such tools must be checked carefully, however, because language programs occasionally state invented 'facts' with complete confidence.

A program for detecting eye disease in people with diabetes, which had been more than ninety per cent accurate in tests, was tried in several clinics in Thailand in 2020. ## The Return of the Electric Car
@INSERT 3
The first modern attempt came in the 1990s. Under pressure from a Californian law requiring manufacturers to offer some vehicles with no exhaust fumes, General Motors produced an electric two-seater, the EV1, in 1996. About 1,100 were built and leased to customers, many of whom became devoted to them. When the law was weakened, however, the company declared the car unprofitable, took the vehicles back and crushed nearly all of them in 2003, to the fury of their drivers.

The turning point is often dated to 2008, when a small Californian firm, Tesla, launched a sports car powered by several thousand of the lithium-ion cells used in laptop computers. It could travel almost 400 kilometres on one charge and accelerate faster than most petrol sports cars. It was expensive and sold in small numbers, but it destroyed the idea that an electric vehicle must be slow and dull, and the established manufacturers began to invest heavily in their own designs.

Part of the appeal lies in simplicity. A petrol engine and its gearbox contain around two thousand moving parts; an electric motor and its drive system have about twenty. There is no oil to change and far less to go wrong, so servicing costs less. An electric car can also recover energy: when the driver slows down, the motor works in reverse as a generator and returns electricity to the battery instead of wasting it as heat in the brakes.

## Printing in Three Dimensions

The spread of the technology beyond industry owes much to the expiry of a patent. Until 2009 the most common printing method was protected, and a machine cost tens of thousands of dollars. Once the patent ran out, small companies and enthusiasts began building simple printers that sold for a few hundred. Designs were shared freely online, and a worldwide community of amateur makers grew up. Builders have begun to print on a much larger scale. A robotic arm or a moving frame squeezes out a special quick-drying concrete, layer upon layer, rather as a baker pipes icing onto a cake. The most ambitious research aims to print living tissue. Instead of plastic, the printer deposits a gel containing human cells, which then grow together. ## The Origins of the Internet
@INSERT 4
The early web consisted mainly of plain text and was used chiefly by academics. That changed in 1993, when a team of students at the University of Illinois released Mosaic, a browser that displayed pictures on the same page as words and could be operated simply by pointing and clicking. It was easy to install, and within eighteen months it had several million users. Businesses realised that the web could reach the general public, and the first online shops and newspapers soon followed.

As the number of pages multiplied, finding anything became the central difficulty. The first guides were lists compiled by hand, which could not keep pace. In 1998 two students at Stanford University, Larry Page and Sergey Brin, founded Google around a new principle. Their search engine ranked a page according to how many other pages linked to it, treating each link as a kind of vote. The results were so much more useful than those of rival services that the company's name became a verb.

The physical side of the internet is easily forgotten. Despite talk of 'the cloud', more than ninety-five per cent of the data passing between continents travels through cables laid on the ocean floor, each no thicker than a garden hose. And access is far from universal: about a third of the world's people, most of them in poorer countries, still do not use the internet at all.

@INSERT 3
Conservation is another field that has been transformed. Counting animals from a light aircraft is expensive and risky; a drone can photograph a colony of seabirds or a herd of elephants without disturbing them, and computer software then counts the individuals. In African reserves, drones fitted with heat-sensitive cameras patrol at night and can pick out poachers from a distance. ## How GPS Knows Where You Are
@INSERT 4
Satellite positioning has uses that its designers never imagined. Fixed receivers anchored to rock can detect movements of a few millimetres a year, allowing geologists to watch continents drift and to measure the strain building up along the faults where earthquakes occur. Biologists attach miniature receivers to animals from albatrosses to whales in order to follow their migrations. And because signals are slowed slightly by moisture in the air, weather services analyse the delays to improve their forecasts.

Yet the system is more fragile than most users realise. The signals arriving from space are extraordinarily faint, comparable to the light of a household bulb seen from 20,000 kilometres away, and they can be drowned out by a transmitter that costs very little. Such jammers are illegal in most countries, but they are used, for example by lorry drivers who do not want their employers to track them. More worrying still is the broadcasting of false signals, which can make a ship's instruments show a position many kilometres from the truth.

There may also be a cost to our own abilities. In experiments, people who followed spoken directions from a device remembered far less about a route than those who used a paper map, and brain scans indicate that the regions involved in finding one's way are less active when a machine does the work. Rescue teams tell of drivers who obeyed their instructions so faithfully that they ended up on mountain tracks, or in rivers.

## Power from the Sun
@INSERT 4
Photovoltaic panels are not the only way of using sunlight. In a concentrated solar power station, thousands of mirrors turn to follow the Sun and reflect its rays onto a receiver at the top of a tower. The intense heat is absorbed by molten salt, which can be kept hot in insulated tanks for many hours and used to make steam for turbines whenever electricity is required. A large plant of this type on the edge of the Sahara in Morocco continues to supply power for several hours after sunset.

Solar energy is having perhaps its greatest effect in places that have never been connected to a power grid. Hundreds of millions of people, most of them in rural Africa and South Asia, still light their homes with kerosene lamps, which are expensive to run, give poor light and fill rooms with harmful smoke. A small panel on the roof, with a battery, can power several lamps, charge a mobile phone and run a radio. Many families buy such kits by paying small sums each week through their phones.

Large solar farms require a great deal of space, and this has led to some inventive solutions. Panels are being floated on reservoirs, where they also reduce the loss of water through evaporation, and raised above fields so that crops can grow or sheep can graze in the partial shade beneath them. A problem for the future is disposal. The first generation of panels is now wearing out, and the industry has only begun to develop ways of recovering the glass, silicon and silver they contain.

## Robots on the Factory Floor
@INSERT 3
Outside the factory, the largest numbers of robots are now found in warehouses. In the huge distribution centres of online retailers, low wheeled machines slide beneath shelving units, lift them and carry them to a human worker, who picks out the item that a customer has ordered. The worker stays in one place, and the shelves come to her. The arrangement illustrates a division of labour that is typical of the present stage of automation: machines do the carrying, while people do whatever requires a skilful hand.

The reason is a puzzle first noted by the robotics researcher Hans Moravec in the 1980s. Tasks that people find difficult, such as playing chess or multiplying large numbers, are comparatively easy for a computer. Things that a small child does without thinking, such as picking up an unfamiliar object, folding a towel or walking over rough ground, turn out to be extraordinarily hard. A robot that can pick ripe strawberries without bruising them at anything like the speed of a human worker has still to be perfected.

This helps to explain why the household robot promised by science fiction has failed to arrive. The one machine that has entered homes in large numbers is the robotic vacuum cleaner, tens of millions of which have been sold since 2002; it succeeds because floors are flat and the task is simple. A general-purpose domestic helper, able to tidy a room or load a dishwasher, would have to cope with an environment far less predictable than any factory.

## The Attention Economy
@INSERT 3
The business of selling attention is older than the internet. In 1833 a young New York printer, Benjamin Day, launched a newspaper called the Sun at a price of one cent, when its rivals cost six. He sold it for less than it cost to produce and made his profit from advertisers, who paid to reach his large readership. To attract readers he filled the paper with crime stories and sensational reports, including a famous series claiming that life had been discovered on the Moon. Radio and television later adopted the same model.

What is new is the precision and persistence of the modern version, and its effect on how people work. Many users believe they can attend to several things at once. Research at Stanford University found the opposite: people who habitually juggled several streams of media performed worse than others on tests of concentration, and, surprisingly, were also slower at switching from one task to another. Other studies of office workers suggest that after an interruption it takes many minutes to return fully to the original task.

Some of the people who built these systems have come to regret them. The designer who invented the endless feed has said publicly that he had not foreseen how much time it would consume and now campaigns for more humane technology. A former executive of a large social network has admitted that its features were deliberately engineered to exploit a weakness in human psychology.

## What Happens to Our Plastic?
@INSERT 3
One proposed alternative is plastic made from plants such as maize or sugar cane rather than from oil. These 'bioplastics' sound attractive, but the label can mislead. Many of them break down only in industrial composting plants, where the temperature is kept at around sixty degrees; in a garden heap or in the sea they last almost as long as ordinary plastic. If they are mixed with conventional plastic waste, moreover, they can spoil the recycled material.

A more surprising possibility emerged in 2016, when Japanese scientists examining samples from a bottle-recycling site discovered a bacterium that had evolved the ability to feed on the plastic used for drinks bottles. The enzymes it produces cut the long molecules into their original components. Researchers in several countries have since modified these enzymes to work many times faster, and a French company is building a plant that will use them to turn old bottles and polyester clothing into new plastic.

Governments, meanwhile, have turned to the law. Rwanda prohibited plastic bags as early as 2008 and searches travellers' luggage for them at the border. The European Union banned a list of throwaway items, including plastic straws, cutlery and cotton buds, in 2021. Even modest measures can be effective: after large shops in England were required to charge a small sum for each carrier bag, the number handed out by the main supermarkets fell by more than ninety per cent.
